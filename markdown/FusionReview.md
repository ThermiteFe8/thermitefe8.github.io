# Plants vs. Zombies Fusion Review
## Intro
Game sequel expectations are a little strange. While [movie sequels](https://www.rottentomatoes.com/m/muppets_most_wanted) do worse than the [original films](https://www.rottentomatoes.com/m/the_muppets), the follow-ups to any given [video game](https://www.metacritic.com/game/portal/) usually get [higher ratings](https://www.metacritic.com/game/portal-2/). I have two explanations. First, technical issues have a greater impact on your ability to play a game than on your ability to watch a film - a CGI glitch on a film is kinda funny, but a buggy section in a game can impede your ability to reach the end. These are usually the first things that get fixed once your team's had the experience of making a full release and games benefit disproportionately from those little bugs/issues. Second, you can't just take audience criticism at face value for a movie - unless you're an action movie, adding more of what people liked about the first movie and removing the stuff people didn't like will only give you a samey disjointed movie.

It also depends on who's developing the game - for the same studio releasing an official sequel, the expectations for a polished experience are usually higher, since those original gamedevs will have learned a lot in the process of developing that original game. For a different studio making a spiritual successor years later, some "unpolish" is forgivable- the goal is more to recreate the complete experience of that original title using modern tools, including some issues that made the original work memorable. For fangames, the scale gets totally flipped - there's _no_ expectations for polish or a cohesive experience, since you're a fan releasing a game for free. Instead, fangames are best received when they introduce novel mechanics and include a _ton_ of content.

On an unrelated note, have you ever heard of Plants vs. Zombies: Fusion?

## Blood, Sweat, and Seeds
Plants vs. Zombies Fusion is a chinese fangame where you combine plants together to make new ones, called fusions. At first, I assumed both the mechanics and art were automated to some degree, similar to the [Pokemon Fusions](https://japeal.com/pkm/) site. After all, there's a lot of plants in the original PvZ - even a half dozen fusions per plant is a big ask from any developer. I was, delightfully, proven wrong. Every single fusion has some unique interaction programmed by [Blue Fly](https://space.bilibili.com/en/3546619314178489), with unique spritework done by [Gfishtus](https://space.bilibili.com/en/85881762), Take Plantern from PvZ. It gets rid of fog. That's it. But in PvZ Fusion, it's the backbone of the Lumos System. Most Plantern fusions get stronger with more light. The [Lumina Cactus](https://pvzfusion.wiki.gg/wiki/Lumina_Cactus) swaps from a needle to a laser that penetrates the entire lane if illuminated with Lumos level 3+. [Lumina Star](https://pvzfusion.wiki.gg/wiki/Lumina_Star) acts like a normal Starfruit at first, but with a little light, the starfruit projectiles auto-target the nearest zombie across any lane. A little more light, and it just perpetually pumps out projectiles, stockpiling them even when there aren't any zombies around, launching all of them simultaneously once a zombie steps on your lawn. If a Plantern fusion doesn't directly benefit from the Lumos system, it's likely a light emitter itself. The [Luminous Pot](https://pvzfusion.wiki.gg/wiki/Luminous_Pot) illuminates whatever you plant on it, saving some space, the [Illumi-nut](https://pvzfusion.wiki.gg/wiki/Illumi-nut) and [Lumina Pumpkin](https://pvzfusion.wiki.gg/wiki/Lumina_Pumpkin) illuminate a 3x3 light area, with both able to heal themselves given enough light, and the [Sea-lamp](https://pvzfusion.wiki.gg/wiki/Sea-lamp) eventually grows to cover a 5x5 area, allowing you to put more firepower on land if your water lanes are well protected. Even better, fusions that don't involve Plantern can still benefit. I didn't learn this during my initial playthrough, since I usually play with my sun producers in front and my lanterns in the back, but if a Sun-Shroom stays illuminated for 150+ seconds, it gets upgraded to a [Firefly-shroom](https://pvz-fusion.fandom.com/wiki/Firefly-shroom), produces even more sun, and continues to benefit from being illuminated.

All of those mechanics stemmed from just one ingredient - you have something similar for essentially every plant. Puff-Shrooms are juicier because you can plant three of them in the same tile. Their fusions are extra efficient since you get three for the price of one. Using a Fume-Shroom or Scaredy-Shroom on a Puff-Shroom will spread them to nearby tiles, multiplying them for free. If you know what you're doing, you can make a bunch of Puff-Shrooms very quickly and have your sun production entirely sorted out before the second zombie shows up.

This isn't even taking into account all of the [Advanced Fusions](https://pvzfusion.wiki.gg/wiki/Fusions/Super) involving 3 plants, the [Titan Fusions](https://pvzfusion.wiki.gg/wiki/Fusions/Titan) that take up multiple tiles, or the Zombie metal fusions that let you combine Zombie items with your plants. You can make a [Football Gatling-Pea](https://pvzfusion.wiki.gg/wiki/Football_Gatling) that shoots knockback peas and occasionally makes Football-type zombies run away from your house or you can make a [Chrono Pea](https://pvzfusion.wiki.gg/wiki/Chrono_Pea) that "erases every trace of its target from the time-space continuum", whatever that means.

You can't automate this - you can only achieve this depth by caring enough about every single fusion to give them unique sprites, animations, and interactions by working tirelessly over the course of a couple of years. This is part of why PvZ Fusion is so admirable - there's so much effort put into a free game from people who have _no_ obligation to continue working on it, especially when compared with Pop Cap's low effort [PVZ Remake](https://youtu.be/VLeCBet48fk?si=02yqgXNoyp71w1k8), which intentionally left out the original developers, implemented the dynamic music system incorrectly, faked a bunch of concept art, and used AI-upscaled sprites. It's amazing what happens when people who genuinely care about PvZ are given the power to make something out of it.

## Teaching Complicated Mechanics
The sheer volume of Fusions seems overwhelming when described on paper, but PvZ Fusion is smart about the way it introduces its mechanics. The Main Campaign is similar to the PvZ's Adventure Mode, with each stage functioning as a tutorial for the mechanics for a basic plant. If you're stuck, you should probably look at the fusions for the last plant you got. While every single fusion has its own unique function, you have _some_ idea of what they'll do based on their individual parts. For example, any fusion involving a Threepeater probably works across multiple lanes and a Spikeweed fusion will probably do something to vehicles - therefore, a Spikeweed Threepeater fusion likely does _something_ to vehicles across multiple lanes.

Additionally, you're encouraged to make as many fusions as possible - every fusion is stronger than the sum of its parts, so you're incentivized to fuse as many plants as possible rather than leaving raw Peashooters and Wall-nuts on the lawn. You end up learning what they do as you experiment with them, which helps you better internalize what each of the Fusions are for than just reading through a wiki or the almanac.

The cleverest item for teaching new mechanics is the Giftbox - you can select it like a plant and it costs 100 Sun. When you place it, the Giftbox spawns a random plant. If you plant it on another plant, it creates a random fusion. For example, if you plant the Giftbox on a squash, you could get _any_ Squash fusion. The Giftbox is unlocked early - towards the end of the Day stages. As a result, it usually spawns plants you haven't unlocked yet, acting as a teaser for upcoming fusions, subtly getting the player excited for upcoming plants while also hinting at their mechanics. More experienced players can start learning ahead if they have a complete defense and a bunch of extra sun before the end of a stage. Even better - it manages to be useful after you've beaten the campaign and unlocked every plant. You can use the Giftbox to save sun on Advanced Fusions - the [Salad-Pult](https://pvzfusion.wiki.gg/wiki/Salad-pult) is a fusion of the Cabbage, Kernel, and Melon-Pult, but you can skip the 300 Sun Melon-Pult by planting a Giftbox on a Cabbage-Pult and Kernel-Pult fusion. Or you can use it to save time - the [Chompzilla](https://pvz-fusion.fandom.com/wiki/Chompzilla) is a fusion of the Chomper, Peashooter, and Wall-Nut, but since the Wall-Nut's cooldown is so slow, you can instead use a Giftbox on a Chomper/Peashooter fusion, since the Giftbox recharges twice as fast.

Even with these powerful fusions, PvZ Fusion is still challenging thanks to the new Zombie variants and adjustable difficulty slider. The difficulty goes all the way from 0 to 5 (comically labeled as "Are you sure?"). To discuss difficulty in PvZ Fusion, I wanna look at two runs - my Difficulty 3 Odyssey run and my Difficulty 5 Main Campaign run.

## On Difficulty
Some background on Odyssey mode - it plays similarly to a Survival stage, where you get to keep the same lawn across multiple rounds, with the zombies ramping up in volume and strength as the rounds go on. You gain access to the [Odyssey Fusions](https://pvzfusion.wiki.gg/wiki/Category:Odyssey_Fusion), which are made up of 4+ plants each. Between each round, you get to pick from a random set of buffs/upgrades - some are specific to certain fusions and some offer extra utility, like the ability to re-select your seeds once a round has started, giving you access to your entire catalogue of plants 24/7. Every few rounds, you can buy a stronger fusion like the [Scylla Leviathan](https://pvzfusion.wiki.gg/wiki/Scylla_Leviathan) and the [Apeacalypse Railgun](https://pvzfusion.wiki.gg/wiki/Apeacalypse_Railgun). You also get to pick your poison - Zombie upgrades can wipe out your plants or force you to face a significantly tougher boss zombie, like the [Giga Football Archduke](https://pvz-fusion.fandom.com/wiki/Giga_Football_Archduke). Part of my enjoyment in this mode comes from looking at the batshit insane Zombie and Plant designs. They look as powerful as they feel - playing this section feels similar to playing hot potato with a nuclear bomb. Making everyone powerful feels like a trick that fades after a while - when everyone's super, [no one will be](https://youtu.be/fmSO2cz2ozQ?si=NnYC5SU5shgKf2QJ). However, even across multiple attempts, it always feels like you're playing with fire whenever you look at someone like the [Abyssal Gargantuar](https://pvz-fusion.fandom.com/wiki/Abyssal_Gargantuar) or the [Panmelonium Cannon](https://pvzfusion.wiki.gg/wiki/Panmelonium_Cannon).

The main challenge in Odyssey is theorycrafting a hyperoptimized defense that uses every tile of your lawn and putting it together before you get overrun by a miniboss with a million health. It's designed with this experience in mind since, at the start, they give you a ton of [Giga-Mecha-Nuts](https://pvzfusion.wiki.gg/wiki/Giga_Mecha-nut), which are important if you want anything that lasts a reasonable amount of time, since they redirect damage to themselves. When playing Odyssey mode, I'd see a plant, figure out how to maximize its DPS, go through the Almanac, find the best supporting plants, and build an entire defense around it, repeating this on and off for multiple weeks until something worked. There's so many fusions and interactions to build a defense around that you never feel stuck, which is why I could commit so much time to this - there's always something else to try out, be it another fusion, another way to get a little extra damage from each projectile, an Odyssey upgrade, or a hidden Zombie metal plant.

The Difficulty 5 Main Campaign run was a different type of challenge. Here, Potato Mines can't even one-shot a Bucket-Head Zombie and they'll sometimes send 4 decently powerful zombies as the first wave instead of a single Basic Zombie. At first, you feel more limited since you no longer have access to any of the Odyssey fusions or upgrades. You don't even have the Advanced Fusions - you need to unlock those all over again. Instead, the Difficulty 5 Run puts emphasis back on the basic fusions. Odyssey runs are cool, but focus a lot on the more obviously powerful fusions that involve 4+ plants - lots of the catalogue gets ignored. There's also more emphasis on the early-game and playing on your feet, rather than making a hyper-optimized defense. In a Difficulty 5 Run, symmetry goes out the window completely - you're barely making it through these waves, since your sun production is stunted by the more aggressive early-game zombies. You gotta use the hammer, glove, zombie glove, and lots of lawnmowers if you want a chance at winning, which is pretty exciting.

Originally, my conclusion was that the Difficulty 5 Run was fun for a while, but eventually got _too_ hard, with no clear way to improve besides "just do the same thing, but slightly faster", making it an optimization of inputs and planting/fusing as efficiently as possible, rather than an optimization of strategy. It doesn't help that the difficulty is blatantly artificial - it's just a health multiplier and wave point increase. Cherry Bombs can't even kill any of the Football Zombie equivalents. However, at some point in the run, something clicked and I wasn't frustrated by the repeated failures/deaths anymore. Even without Odyssey plants, there's more than enough basic fusions to try out if you're stuck. Sometimes, the solution's in an overlooked plant, like the [Squash-Spreader](https://pvz-fusion.fandom.com/wiki/Squash-spreader), which carried me through a lot of the pool stages, or the [Orca-Shroom](https://pvz-fusion.fandom.com/wiki/Whale-shroom), which you manually trigger using spare Sea-Shrooms. Instead, my takeaway's that unfair, clearly artificial challenges can be fun, so long as I'm attached to the learning process and struggle itself rather than the reward at the end.

## Conclusion
Plants vs. Zombies Fusion is built on an inhuman dedication to every single plant and they're _still adding more_. Every night, I get a notification from the Fusion discord about a new monstrosity to look forward to whenever the next update drops. There's more that I didn't talk about, like an open-world style mode that I don't even understand and a whole campaign where they recreated the Bloons TD mechanics using PvZ fusion mechanics. Even though there's some issues with the difficulty slider making the intended experience unclear, it's easy to to ignore when there's so much love poured into every other aspect of this fangame. If you wish any effort at all were put into the PvZ remake, enjoy difficult strategy games, and like solving optimization problems, give this game a shot.
